## Spin Coating Time PVK 2
### Solvent evaporation window and crystal growth
1. **Too short (e.g. 30 s)**
- Significant solvent remains when spinning stops. Afterward, the film stands still while solvent continues migrating and evaporating.
- This post-spin drying causes uncontrolled shrinkage and nucleation, which generates cracks, pores, and a mosaic of grain sizes.
- Mean gray level tends to be **medium (around 60–90)** but highly non-uniform.
2. **Optimal (e.g. 35–30 s at 5000 rpm)**
- The majority of solvent is removed during the controlled spin environment.
- By the time spinning ends, the film is already in a quasi-solid perovskite-rich state, so post-spin deformations are small.
- Result: a compact, dark film (gray **0–40 or 40–65**) with high coverage.
3. **Too long (e.g. 40 s)**
- The film dries too much during spinning; concentration and supersaturation climb very early.
- You obtain a large number of small nuclei that cannot ripen due to quick immobilization; the final film is composed of fine grains with a high density of grain boundaries and micropores.
- Gray level can sit around **90**, with a matte appearance (many internal scattering centers).
### Device metrics
- **Jsc**
- Too short spin time 2 → micro-cracks and voids reduce the effective absorption path and create recombination hot spots; Jsc declines.
- Too long → micro-porous, fine-grained film; optical absorption per thickness can decrease due to scattering and poor packing, and carrier extraction worsens; Jsc also falls.
- **Voc & FF**
- Both extremes increase SRH recombination (via grain-boundary and surface traps) and lower Rsh.
- Optimal time yields minimized grain boundary area per volume and fewer deep traps, which is favorable for high Voc and FF.
Overall, spin time 2 determines **how much of the crystallization happens under controlled dynamic conditions vs uncontrolled static drying**.
