## Antisolvent Dropping Timing
### Critical timing window relative to spin
During PVK2, the film evolves from **solution-rich** to **gel-like intermediate** and finally to **solid perovskite**. The **dripping time** (e.g. 15 s, 10 s, 6–8 s before the end of spin) decides *which stage* is perturbed by antisolvent.
1. **Too early dripping (e.g. 15 s before end)**
- The film is still very dilute; antisolvent collapses low-concentration solution into small, disconnected nuclei.
- Strong hydrodynamic shear at that moment further tears the forming network apart.
- Morphology: coverage can be as low as **2–10%**, with large bright areas (gray **90–120**) corresponding to bare or ultra-thin regions.
2. **Optimal dripping (e.g. 10 s or 6–8 s before end)**
- The solution is partially concentrated but still mobile; antisolvent now triggers a sharp but *coherent* nucleation and growth across the film.
- The intermediate adduct phase transforms uniformly into the black perovskite phase.
- Coverage >**89–100%**, gray level **0–40** or **40–65**, and surface is comparatively smooth.
3. **Too late dripping**
- Spontaneous crystallization has already started; the film is partially solid. Antisolvent now mainly affects the top layer, creating a multi-layered structure of grain sizes and phases.
- That gives rise to vertical inhomogeneity and mixed phases, increasing interface and bulk traps.
### Device metrics
- **Too early**
- Massive pinholes and voids → devastating for Rsh and Jsc, so PCE often collapses.
- **Optimal time**
- Produces dense, uniform films with reduced defect density; both Voc and FF benefit from lower nonradiative recombination and better Rsh.
- **Too late**
- Leads to a vertical stack of different grain sizes and trap densities; carriers encounter additional recombination at intra-layer interfaces, lowering Voc and FF.
Antisolvent timing is thus a **kinetic knob** controlling when nucleation occurs along the spin trajectory.
