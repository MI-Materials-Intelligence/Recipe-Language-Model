## Spin Coating Speed PVK 1
### Role in film formation
Spin step 1 is primarily a **wetting and leveling stage**:
- It controls **how far the solution spreads** before strong thinning occurs.
- It sets the spatial distribution of solute before antisolvent triggers crystallization.
1. **Too low speed (e.g. 600 rpm)**
- The liquid spreads slowly; the center retains a thick puddle, while the edge is thin.
- Solvent evaporation is non-uniform: the center reaches supersaturation later than the edges. This leads to radial gradients in nucleation time and grain size.
- Morphologically:
- Coverage may be only **40–60%** uniform; thick/dark central regions (gray 0–40) coexist with thin/bright edges (gray 90–120).
- Height gradients drive crack formation during drying.
2. **Optimal speed (~900–1000 rpm)**
- Enough centrifugal force to spread the liquid across the entire substrate while still leaving a relatively thick, uniform wet film.
- Solvent evaporation is more homogeneous, so the entire area hits supersaturation within a narrower time window → more uniform nucleation.
- Coverage can then reach **~99.9–100%** under appropriate PVK2 and antisolvent conditions.
3. **Too high speed (1100–1200 rpm)**
- The solution is flung off the substrate before it can fully wet the surface; the center may be nearly dry when antisolvent arrives.
- You get patchy wetting and wet–dry boundaries serving as nucleation sites for rims and holes.
- Effective coverage can drop to ~**30%**, with strong contrast between thick residual zones (dark) and exposed substrate (bright).
### Device metrics
- **Jsc**
- Sensitive to the *uniform* thickness and coverage: too low or too high PVK1 speed both lead to large uncovered or under-crystallized regions → Jsc decreases.
- **Voc**
- Non-uniform thickness and coverage generate spatially varying band bending and trap densities. Regions with ultrathin perovskite allow field penetration and interface recombination; thicker regions accumulate strain and defects. Both reduce Voc.
- **FF**
- PVK1 that is too low or too high speed yields macroscopic inhomogeneity, which increases both Rs (percolation through tortuous paths) and local shunts (pinholes where ETL and HTL can contact). This lowers Rsh and FF even when average thickness is similar.
A **moderate PVK1 speed** is crucial mainly to ensure a homogeneous starting wet layer; it does not by itself define final thickness, but it determines whether PVK2 + antisolvent can ever give you a compact, dark (gray 0–40) film.
